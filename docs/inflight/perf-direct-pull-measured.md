# Direct pull, finished and measured: it is 3x FASTER at ten workers and 20x slower at five thousand

<!-- inflight-type: perf -->
<!-- inflight-impact: performance -->
<!-- inflight-labels: needs-measurement -->

Opened 2026-08-22. **The 2022 direct-pull engine, rebuilt on current code with the blocking wait it
never had, and measured against the shipped engine - same broker, same topic, same 500,000 records,
repeats at every point, both arms alternating.**

It settles the one objection that survived the retraction in
[`parked-2022-central-queue-rework.md`](parked-2022-central-queue-rework.md) - direct pull turns shard
access from single-consumer into N-way concurrent, and that cost grows with concurrency - and settles
it in a shape nobody predicted: **the cost is real and grows without bound, and below about a hundred
workers the design is not slower at all, it is three times faster than what ships.**

Branch `perf/direct-pull-measured`. **Not a merge candidate** - the deliverable is the number and the
cause.

## The numbers

`bench/run-direct-pull.sh`, which drives `bench/run-bisect.sh` **one invocation per point with both
arms inside it**, so baseline and direct pull at the same delay and concurrency are measured within a
minute of each other, and the arm that goes first alternates between repeats. Same broker
(`pc-bench-broker`), same topic (`bench-500000-p10`, ten partitions), **500,000 records every time**,
`UNORDERED`, a fresh consumer group per run, logging pinned by `bench/conf`. Medians; three runs per
cell in the delay sweep, six in the concurrency sweep.

| delay | concurrency | shipped engine | direct pull | delta | peak in flight (shipped / direct) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0ms | 1,000 | **106,769** | **14,329** | **-86.6%** | 1,000 / 1,000 |
| 0ms | 5,000 | **92,661** | **4,416** | **-95.2%** | 1,702 / 4,744 |
| 2ms | 1,000 | **65,885** | **8,969** | **-86.4%** | 514 / 999 |
| 2ms | 5,000 | **28,575** | **4,610** | **-83.9%** | 303 / 4,645 |
| 100ms | 1,000 | **8,862** | **8,334** | **-5.9%** | 1,000 / 1,000 |
| 100ms | 5,000 | **19,478** | **4,721** | **-75.8%** | 2,742 / 4,996 |

**Delay behaves exactly as the harness's own README predicts** - at 100ms the sleep dominates and at
concurrency 1,000 the engines are 6% apart, which is why a single measurement at a long handler would
have found nothing. It is at 0ms, where the number is almost entirely framework overhead, that the
engines separate.

**But delay is not where the result is. Concurrency is.**

### The concurrency sweep, at 0ms - and it changes sign

| concurrency | shipped engine | direct pull | delta |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 | 26,869 | **86,179** | **+220.7%** |
| 100 | 78,852 | 76,715 | -2.7% |
| 250 | 94,462 | 65,807 | -30.3% |
| 500 | 103,726 | 39,461 | -62.0% |
| 1,000 | 106,769 | 14,329 | -86.6% |
| 5,000 | 92,661 | 4,416 | -95.2% |

**Direct pull's throughput is a strictly decreasing function of concurrency** - 86,179, 76,715,
65,807, 39,461, 14,329, 4,416. Every worker added makes it slower. The shipped engine does the
opposite: it climbs from 26,869 to 106,769 and then levels off.

**And below the crossover, at about a hundred workers, direct pull wins outright.** At concurrency 10
it is **3.2x faster** than the shipped engine, and it beats every number the shipped engine produces
at *any* setting - while holding **ten** records in flight instead of a thousand.

Run-to-run spread is itself a result: **shipped engine 0.5% to 30%; direct pull 3% to 67%.** The
design is not only concurrency-sensitive, it is unstable - what a contended structure looks like.

**The machine.** Shared, and busy with other agent sessions: the one-minute load recorded before each
point ranged from **7** to **860** - but most of the high readings are load *generated by the arm
under test*, which is itself part of the finding. Two controls stop that reading as a confound. First,
every shipped-engine cell reproduces numbers taken independently in an earlier session on the same
machine and topic (106,769 against ~105,000-107,000 at 0ms/1,000; 65,885 against ~65,000-67,000 at
2ms/1,000; 8,862 against ~8,750-8,875 at 100ms/1,000; 19,478 against ~19,600-19,900 at 100ms/5,000).
Second, the two arms are adjacent in time at every point and alternate which goes first. **Machine
drift moves both arms; it does not move one of them by a factor of twenty.**

## The question, answered

**Does N-way concurrent shard access cost anything?** Yes, and it is the dominant term in the engine
above about a hundred workers.

**Does the cost grow with concurrency?** Yes, and more sharply than "grows" suggests: **direct pull's
throughput is a strictly decreasing function of concurrency.** Every extra worker makes the system
slower. The shipped engine does the opposite - it gets faster from 10 to 1,000 workers and then
levels off.

Below that crossover the sign flips, and the win is not marginal: at ten workers direct pull is
**3.2x faster than the shipped engine**, and it beats every number the shipped engine produces at
*any* concurrency while holding ten records in flight instead of a thousand. What direct pull removes
at low concurrency is the control loop's dispatch latency - one round trip per batch, with a 1ms
sleep in every pass - which is what bounds the shipped engine when there are few workers to feed.

**So this is not a slow design. It is a design whose cost and whose benefit are both per-worker, and
they cross.** The 2022 branch never found that out, because neither the phase that was direct pull
nor the phase that produced the "1/3 as fast" number was in a state to be measured.

**A warning that comes out of the same table:** direct pull holds *more* records in flight than the
shipped engine at every high-concurrency point - 4,996 against 2,742 at 100ms/5,000, comfortably past
the [platform-thread ceiling](perf-platform-threads-are-the-ceiling.md) the shipped engine stops at -
while delivering a quarter of the throughput. Under that note's own law,
`peak in-flight = min(maxConcurrency, rate x residence time)`, a longer residence time raises
in-flight at a *lower* rate. **A higher peak-in-flight number is not evidence of a better engine**,
and this is the cleanest counter-example the harness has produced.

## What the 2022 branch actually did - including a correction to the retraction

Read commit by commit, `origin/refactor/gpt3-central-queue-direct-pull` is not one design, it is several
in sequence, and the retraction in
[`parked-2022-central-queue-rework.md`](parked-2022-central-queue-rework.md) attributes the busy-spin
to the wrong one.

`PCWorker`'s live line moved three times across the branch:

| Commits | Worker's live path | Commit subjects |
|---|---|---|
| `c167b94b0` .. `5dcd39bb3` | `acquireFromWm()` -> `QueuedWorkManager.take()` -> **`QueuedShardManager.inner()`**, the busy-spin | "step", "bad", "START: use a queue of queues", "START: ThreadLocal attempt" |
| `59ff8cc3a` .. `5469262a1` | neither - the worker had its own inbox | "runs extremely slow", "runs correctly, fast, but not as fast" |
| `58826c349` .. `7e775a111` | **`centralQueue.take()`**, a `LinkedBlockingQueue<Batch>` filled by the control loop | "works", **"still only 1/3~ a fast as ThreadPoolExecutor version"** |

**So the "~1/3 as fast" verdict was recorded on a commit where the busy-spin was already dead code.**
`QueuedShardManager` was still constructed by `PCModule` and still the `ShardManager` for record
registration, but nothing ever called its `take()`. What that commit measured was a *central queue* -
an intermediate buffer with a correct blocking wait, just a different buffer from the executor's.

That does not rescue the verdict, it moves the reason. The design that commit measured **was not
direct pull at all**: workers still received work that the control loop had selected and pushed. It
therefore says nothing about N-way concurrent shard access either, and its own `CentralQueue` had two
defects that would have shown up the moment anything failed - an unsynchronised `TreeSet` retry queue
shared between the control loop and every worker, and a `getAtMostFromRetryQueue` that loops forever
when the first entry's retry delay has not passed.

**The busy-spin retraction stands, and applies to the earlier phase.** During `c167b94b0`..`5dcd39bb3`
the worker's idle path really was `while (element == null) element = tryOneIterationQueueRemoval();`,
and those commits are labelled "step", "bad" and "runs extremely slow". The branch abandoned direct
pull *before* it produced a number, and then produced a number for something else.

**Neither phase measured the architecture. That is what this note fixes.**

## What was built instead

The smallest thing that answers the question, on current code:
[`DirectPullWorkerPool`](../../parallel-consumer-core/src/main/java/bz/stub/parallelconsumer/internal/DirectPullWorkerPool.java),
selected by `ParallelConsumerOptions.directPullEngine` (defaulting from the `pc.directPull` system
property so the benchmark harness can pick the engine per run without a template change).

`maxConcurrency` worker loops occupy the existing pool's threads. Each calls
`WorkManager.getWorkIfAvailable(batchSize)` itself. The control loop stops distributing work
entirely - no `checkPipelinePressure()`, no `isPoolQueueLow()`, no `getQueueTargetLoaded()`, no
`getNumberOfUserFunctionsQueued()`, nothing submitted to the executor's queue.

**The blocking wait, which is the part the 2022 branch never wrote.** No spin and no timed poll:

1. a worker reads a version counter, **then** scans the shards, lock-free;
2. finding nothing, it takes a lock and compares that version against the current one - a producer
   that made work available in between has already bumped it, so the worker rescans rather than
   parking;
3. otherwise it `await`s, releasing the lock atomically.

Producers bump the version **before** signalling, and signal at most as many workers as there is
selectable work - which under an ordered mode is bounded by the shard count, not by the number of
records queued behind each shard head (`ShardManager.getUpperBoundOnSelectableWork`). The `await` has
a five-second bound as a safety net for work that becomes selectable through the passage of time
alone - a retry delay expiring - and nothing in a throughput run ever reaches it.

**Three pieces of shared state had to change, and each is a real cost of the design, not an
incidental fix:**

- `WorkContainer.inFlight` becomes a CAS, because "is it free? then take it" has to be one
  indivisible step once every worker scans the same shard. A lost claim is skipped, not
  double-delivered.
- `WorkManager.numberRecordsOutForProcessing` becomes atomic - incremented by whichever worker takes
  work, decremented by the control loop, and it gates the broker poller.
- `ShardManager.iterationResumePoint` becomes volatile.

Under the shipped engine all three are uncontended: the control loop is the only selector, so the CAS
never loses and the counter is never contended.

**What direct pull does NOT remove, contrary to the claim in the parked note.** `DynamicLoadFactor`
survives. It has two jobs, and only one of them is about the executor queue: it also sizes the buffer
of *polled records sitting in the shards*, which is what `WorkManager.isSufficientlyLoaded()` uses to
pause the broker poller. That buffer does not disappear when the executor queue does. So the factor is
still there, stepped up off a starvation signal from the workers instead of off
`ThreadPoolExecutor.getQueue().size()`. Pinning it at its initial value instead would have measured
buffer depths rather than engines.

**What direct pull genuinely removes is the executor-queue reading**, `getNumberOfUserFunctionsQueued()`
-> `workerThreadPool.getQueue().size()` - and that is the one that matters for
[virtual threads](perf-platform-threads-are-the-ceiling.md), because a virtual-thread executor does
not expose it. That part of the argument survives the measurement intact.

`ExternalEngine` opts out: its worker pool is one thread, with the concurrency in the external runtime.

## Why it is slower above the crossover - stated as a prediction before it was tested

**The mechanism is not the CAS and not the lock. It is that a shard's selection is a linear scan from
the head, and direct pull pays that scan per record where the shipped engine pays it per batch.**

`ProcessingShard.getWorkIfAvailable` walks `entries` from the lowest offset. Entries are removed on
success, **not** when taken, so every record currently in flight is still sitting in that map, ahead
of the next selectable one, and under `UNORDERED` the walk does not stop at an unavailable entry - it
skips it (running `addToSlowWorkMaybe`, an `Instant` subtraction and a `HashSet` insert per skip) and
carries on.

With `C` records in flight spread over `S` shards, finding one selectable record costs about `C/S`
skipped entries. The shipped engine calls `getWorkIfAvailable(delta)` **once** and gets up to `delta`
records out of that one walk. Direct pull calls it once per record, from every worker, concurrently.

**The prediction, written before the low-concurrency arm was run:** the deficit should scale with
`C/S`, so it should be roughly proportional to concurrency, and it should nearly vanish once
concurrency is small enough that `C/S` approaches 1. At ten partitions that means a large deficit at
1,000 and 5,000, a modest one at 100, and almost none at 10.

**It held, and the sign change is the strongest form the confirmation could have taken.** At
concurrency 10, where `C/S` is 1, direct pull is not merely close - it is **3.2x faster**, because
with `C/S = 1` there is nothing to skip and the design's *benefit* (no control-loop round trip per
batch) is all that is left. The deficit then appears exactly where the skip distance appears, and
grows with it: parity at 100, -30% at 250, -62% at 500, -87% at 1,000, -95% at 5,000. **A constant
overhead cannot produce that curve; a per-record cost proportional to in-flight-per-shard can, and
does.**

**This also explains a result that would otherwise contradict it.** The hypothesis register's row 7 -
*"the in-shard rescan past in-flight records costs throughput"* - was tested on the shipped engine
and **refuted twice**: making dispatch ten times cheaper moved end-to-end throughput by 0% at 0ms,
2ms and 100ms. Both results are correct. The same walk is off the critical path when it is amortised
across a batch on one thread, and dominant when it is paid per record on every thread. **A cost that
does not matter in one engine is not a cost that does not matter.**

**And the two other observations agree with it rather than with a lock-contention story.** Direct pull
holds *more* records in flight than the shipped engine at the same setting while delivering a fraction
of the throughput - it is not starved, it is busy - and the JVM burns eight-plus cores at
concurrency 5,000 with a zero-cost handler, which is scanning, not blocking.

## What is not implemented, and whether it could change the answer

**Yes, one of them could change it completely.**

- **O(1) shard selection is not implemented, and it is the whole finding.** A shard that could hand
  back its next selectable record without walking past the in-flight ones would remove the term above
  entirely. Nothing about direct pull requires a linear scan; it is what `ProcessingShard` happens to
  do today, and the shipped engine hides the cost by amortising it. **A direct-pull engine is not
  viable on this shard representation, and the measurement here does not say a direct-pull engine on a
  different one would be slow.** That is the experiment worth running next, and it is a bigger change
  than this one.
- **Batched claims are not implemented.** A worker could claim K records per scan and process them one
  at a time, amortising the walk the same way the control loop does. Deliberately left out: claiming K
  marks K records in flight at once, so peak in-flight and the meaning of `maxConcurrency` both change,
  and the arm would no longer be comparable.
- **Per-worker shard affinity is not implemented** - the 2022 branch's `ThreadLocal` and
  "queue of queues" commits were reaching for exactly this. It would cut both the scan and the
  contention, at the cost of fairness and of rebalancing shards between workers.
- **Retry-heavy workloads are not measured.** Every run here succeeds on first delivery, so
  `RetryQueue`'s write lock - which `ProcessingShard.getWorkIfAvailable` takes on *every* call once the
  queue is non-empty - never fires. Under failures that lock becomes a global serialisation point taken
  once per record instead of once per batch, so the deficit should be **worse**, not better.
- **Only `UNORDERED` at ten partitions was swept.** `KEY` ordering has a different shape - many shards,
  one entry each, and the walk breaks at the first entry - so the scan term should be far smaller. It
  was not swept, and the one ordered test that exists is discussed below.

## What the test suite said

The core unit suite was run with `-Dpc.directPull=true`: **369 tests, three distinct failures, none of
them a correctness violation.** No ordering breakage, no double delivery, no offset corruption, no
stall. Each failure is an assertion that encodes the pre-loaded-queue design:

- `ParallelEoSStreamProcessorPauseResumeTest.testThatInFlightWorkIsFinishedSuccessfullyAndOffsetsAreCommitted`
  (all three commit modes) asserts that after `pauseIfRunning()` **more than** `maxConcurrency` records
  still complete - i.e. that work already pushed into the executor queue keeps running. Its own comment
  says "exact number is based on dynamic load factor". Direct pull has no such queue, so exactly the
  in-flight records finish and the count is exactly `maxConcurrency`. **Pause becomes exact.** That is a
  behaviour improvement the test cannot express.
- `ParallelEoSStreamProcessorTest.offsetsAreNeverCommittedForMessagesStillInFlightShort` asserts that
  the instant the *first* handler starts, **both** records are already out for processing. That holds
  when one control-loop pass dispatches the whole batch; under direct pull each worker claims its own
  record, so the second may not be claimed yet. A dispatch-granularity assumption, not a defect.
- `ParallelEoSStreamProcessorTest.lessKeysThanThreads` timed out at 3 minutes when the machine was at
  load ~100, and **passed when the same class was re-run at load ~12**. Not reproducible on an
  unloaded machine, so it is recorded as a load artefact rather than a finding. What is worth keeping
  from it: at load ~100 it ran at ~900 msg/s against the shipped engine's ~1,786 msg/s on the same
  test, both control-loop-latency-bound - but those two numbers were taken under very different load
  and **must not be quoted as a measurement**.

### Correction, 2026-08-22: the count above is wrong, and one of the failures was not on the list

Re-run on `test/direct-pull-coverage` at a one-minute load of 8-13, this section's claim was checked
rather than inherited. **The suite is 373 tests, not 369, and it produced FOUR distinct failing
methods, not three.** Two of the three above reproduced. The third did not, and a fourth appeared
that this section does not mention.

- The pause and the dispatch-granularity failures **reproduced exactly as described**, and both are
  correctly diagnosed here. They are now fixed in the tests rather than in the engine: the
  dispatch-granularity one awaits the converged state instead of reading it at the instant the first
  handler starts (its assertion is unchanged - still exactly 0 awaiting and exactly 2 out), and the
  pause one is split, with the "strictly more than `maxConcurrency`" claim moved into
  `pausingDrainsThePreLoadedExecutorQueueAsWellAsTheInFlightRecords`, where it keeps its original
  strength for the shipped engine and is visibly skipped for direct pull.
- **`lessKeysThanThreads` did not fail at all**, which is consistent with the load-artefact reading.
- **`OrderingModeDispatchParityTest.keyAndUnorderedCostTheSameToDispatch` failed - on BOTH engines.**
  It also fails on the default engine on the same box, so it is not a direct-pull failure at all and
  belongs in the flake ledger, where it now is.
- **A fourth failure, which is the one that matters:**
  `pausingAndResumingProcessingShouldWork` reported **1001 records processed where 1000 were
  produced**. That is one extra call to the user function, and this section's headline claim of "no
  double delivery" cannot be made on the evidence available. It did not reproduce in 11 attempts and
  is recorded in
  [`test-untracked-ci-flakes.md`](test-untracked-ci-flakes.md) with what was and was not ruled out.

**So the sentence "369 tests, three distinct failures, none of them a correctness violation" should
be read as: a suite run on a busy machine, counted once, with the failures triaged by inspection
rather than by re-running them.** Selection itself is now covered - see `DirectPullConcurrentSelectionTest`,
`DirectPullWorkerPoolTest` and `DirectPullEngineParityTest`, each mutation-tested - and no correctness
violation has been reproduced. But "the suite passed except for three assertions about the old design"
was a stronger claim than the run supported.

## Reproducing it

```sh
./mvnw install -DskipTests -Dcopyright.skip=true
BENCH_SKIP_PRODUCE=1 BENCH_TOPIC=bench-500000-p10 BENCH_PARTITIONS=10 \
  bench/run-direct-pull.sh 500000 3                      # the delay sweep
DELAYS=0 CONCURRENCIES="10 100 250 500" \
BENCH_SKIP_PRODUCE=1 BENCH_TOPIC=bench-500000-p10 BENCH_PARTITIONS=10 \
  bench/run-direct-pull.sh 500000 3                      # the concurrency sweep
```

The engine is selected per run by the `core-dp` mode, which `bench/run-bisect.sh` translates into
`-Dpc.directPull=true` - a system property rather than a harness argument because
`Bench.java.template` is compiled against every released version in the sweep and none of them has
the option.

## What to do with this

1. **Do not merge this branch.** It is a measurement, and on the shard representation PC has today
   the design loses badly at the concurrencies this project's users actually configure.
2. **The next experiment is O(1) shard selection**, not another engine. Both engines walk past
   in-flight records to find work; the shipped one hides it by amortising, direct pull cannot. Fix
   the walk and the two arms are worth comparing again - and that fix would benefit the shipped
   engine at high concurrency too, where its own throughput has started to fall (92,661 at
   concurrency 5,000 against 106,769 at 1,000).
   **Caution: that walk is load-bearing** - the hypothesis register records that in-flight records
   remaining in the shard is *how ordering is enforced*, discovered by removing them and watching ten
   tests fail.
3. **The virtual-threads argument survives intact and is not about throughput.** What direct pull
   removes for certain is `getNumberOfUserFunctionsQueued()` reaching into
   `ThreadPoolExecutor.getQueue().size()`, which is what
   [blocks adopting virtual threads](perf-platform-threads-are-the-ceiling.md). That is worth having
   whether or not this engine ever is - and it can be had without this engine, by moving the pressure
   system onto PC's own accounting.
4. **Low-concurrency users are being left throughput on the table by the control loop's dispatch
   latency**, and that is a finding about the *shipped* engine, not about direct pull. At
   concurrency 10 the shipped engine reaches 26,869 msg/s where the same machine, same records, and a
   worker that helps itself reaches 86,179.
